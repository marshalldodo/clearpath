Lecture Note: Defining “Scam”

1. Core Definition

A scam is a deliberate and deceptive scheme designed to obtain money, property, sensitive information, or some other benefit from a person or organization by gaining their trust through false pretenses, misrepresentation, or manipulation.

In short: a scam is a planned deception used for illicit gain.

2. Key Elements of a Scam

A scam typically involves:

1. Intentional deception — the perpetrator knows the claim or offer is false.

2. Victim reliance — the target believes the false claim and acts on it.

3. Illicit gain — the scammer seeks money, data, goods, access, or influence.

4. Trust exploitation — scammers often impersonate authority, familiarity, opportunity, or urgency.

5. Concealment — the true nature of the scheme is hidden from the victim.

3. Scam vs. Fraud

· Scam is a broad, everyday term for a deceptive scheme or “trick.”

· Fraud is often a legal term for intentional deception causing loss or gain.

· Not every scam is prosecuted as fraud, but many scams are fraudulent in nature.

4. Common Forms

· Phishing and smishing

· Fake investment or “get-rich-quick” schemes

· Romance scams

· Advance-fee fraud

· Impersonation scams

· Online marketplace scams

· Tech-support scams

· Lottery or prize scams

5. Typical Red Flags

· Urgency or pressure to act immediately

· Requests for secrecy

· Unexpected prizes, refunds, or inheritances

· Requests for gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, or remote access

· Poor grammar or mismatched email/website addresses

· Offers that seem too good to be true

· Requests for personal or financial information from unsolicited contacts

6. Key Takeaway

A scam is not merely a bad deal or a legitimate business failure; it is a deceptive scheme intended to exploit trust for gain. The defining feature is intentional manipulation of the victim’s beliefs or actions. 1. Core Definition

Phishing is a form of social engineering in which an attacker impersonates a trustworthy entity—such as a bank, employer, government agency, colleague, or popular service—to trick a person into revealing sensitive information, clicking a malicious link, downloading malware, or authorizing a fraudulent action.

In short: phishing is deception delivered through a fake communication channel to capture credentials, data, money, or access.

2. Key Elements

1. Impersonation — the attacker poses as someone legitimate.

2. Deceptive channel — usually email, SMS, phone, social media, or fake websites.

3. Psychological manipulation — urgency, fear, curiosity, authority, or reward.

4. Victim action — clicking a link, opening an attachment, entering credentials, approving a payment, or granting access.

5. Attacker objective — credentials, financial data, malware installation, account takeover, or further compromise.

3. How Phishing Works

A typical sequence:

1. Attacker selects a target or group.

2. Crafts a believable message using logos, names, and context.

3. Delivers it via email, text, call, or social media.

4. Directs the victim to a fake login page or malicious attachment.

5. Captures credentials, installs malware, or induces a payment.

6. Uses the obtained access for fraud, data theft, or further attacks.

4. Common Types

· Email phishing — mass fraudulent emails.

· Spear phishing — targeted at a specific person or role.

· Whaling — targets senior executives.

· Smishing — phishing via SMS.

· Vishing — voice phishing by phone.

· Clone phishing — a legitimate message is copied and resent with malicious links.

· Pharming — redirecting users to fake websites without their knowledge.

· Business email compromise (BEC) — impersonating an executive or vendor to authorize payments.

5. Red Flags

· Mismatched sender address or domain

· Urgent demands or threats

· Unexpected requests for credentials or payment

· Suspicious links or attachments

· Generic greetings in supposedly personal messages

· Requests to bypass normal procedures

· Login pages reached through unsolicited links

· Poor spelling, grammar, or unusual tone

6. Phishing vs. Scam

· Scam is the broad category of deceptive schemes for gain.

· Phishing is a specific scam technique: deception through a fake communication to steal information or access.

· All phishing is a scam; not all scams are phishing.

7. Key Takeaway

Phishing is not just a fake email; it is a trust-exploitation attack that uses impersonation and manipulation to make the victim perform an unsafe action. 1.2 Taxonomy of Deceptive Schemes

Purpose: Provide a structured classification so diverse schemes can be compared systematically.

Classification dimensions:

· By channel: face-to-face, phone, email, SMS, social media, web, hybrid.

· By target: mass-market, targeted (spear), executive (whaling), vulnerable populations.

· By motive: financial gain, data theft, account takeover, espionage, disruption.

· By mechanism: impersonation, advance-fee, romance grooming, fake investment, malware delivery.

· By duration: one-shot, staged/multi-phase, long-term grooming (e.g., pig-butchering).

· By actor: lone criminal, organized group, state-sponsored, insider.

Teaching point: A single incident can sit at multiple intersections (e.g., a spear-phishing email delivering ransomware to a CFO is targeted, email-based, financial, and multi-phase).

1.3 Victim–Attacker Interaction Models

Purpose: Model the sequence of trust-building and exploitation.

Common models:

· Lure → Hook → Capture → Exploit → Cash-out — maps the attack lifecycle.

· Trust cycle: reconnaissance → pretext establishment → compliance request → extraction → cover-up.

· Victim decision pathway: exposure → attention → interpretation → action → consequence.

Teaching point: These models help identify intervention points — where detection, training, or technical controls can break the chain.

1.4 Economic Models of Scams

Purpose: Explain why scams persist and scale.

Key concepts:

· Low cost, high volume: email is nearly free; response rates can be tiny yet profitable.

· Asymmetric economics: attacker cost vs. victim loss vs. defender cost.

· Repeat victimization: successful victims are re-targeted (sucker lists).

· Market structures: phishing-as-a-service, scam call centers, money-mule networks.

· Rational choice perspective: offenders weigh effort, risk, reward.

Teaching point: Economics explains the persistence of "obviously fake" messages — they filter for the most susceptible victims.

2. Social Engineering and Human Factors

2.1 Psychological Principles

Purpose: Identify the levers attackers pull to bypass rational decision-making.

Principle How It Is Used

Authority Impersonating police, banks, executives, IT support.

Urgency "Act within 24 hours or your account will be closed."

Scarcity Limited-time offers, limited spots.

Fear Threats of legal action, account suspension, arrest.

Reciprocity Small favors or gifts create obligation.

Social proof "Thousands have already claimed this."

Liking/familiarity Mimicking colleagues, friends, or brands.

Commitment/consistency Small initial compliance escalates to larger requests.

Teaching point: These are the same principles used in legitimate persuasion and marketing — context and intent distinguish manipulation from influence.

2.2 Pretexting

Purpose: Understand how attackers construct a believable false scenario.

Elements of a pretext:

· Identity — who the attacker claims to be.

· Story — a plausible reason for contact.

· Authority/credibility cues — logos, jargon, caller-ID spoofing, references.

· Request — the action the victim is asked to take.

· Time pressure — limiting the victim’s ability to verify.

Examples: fake IT support asking for a password reset; "vendor" requesting updated bank details; "police officer" demanding payment.

2.3 Cognitive Biases

Purpose: Explain systematic errors in judgment that increase susceptibility.

· Optimism bias: "It won't happen to me."

· Confirmation bias: interpreting ambiguous cues as consistent with expectations.

· Anchoring: first offer or claim shapes subsequent judgment.

· Halo effect: professionalism or branding implies legitimacy.

· Sunk-cost fallacy: victims continue after partial loss to recover investment.

· Normalcy bias: underestimating unusual events.

2.4 Victim Demographics and Vulnerability

Purpose: Move beyond stereotypes and understand risk factors.

Factors studied:

· Age: mixed findings — older adults may be more susceptible to some scams; younger adults to others (e.g., online marketplace).

· Digital literacy: lower skill correlates with higher click rates in some studies.

· Emotional state: loneliness, stress, grief, and financial pressure increase vulnerability.

· Isolation: lack of second opinions or verification support.

· Life events: divorce, bereavement, job loss are exploited in romance and investment scams.

· Overconfidence: high self-assessed security awareness can reduce caution.

Teaching point: Vulnerability is situational, not fixed — anyone can be targeted at the right moment.

2.5 Insider Threats and Human Error

Purpose: Recognize that organizational compromise often begins with an employee action.

· Accidental insiders: well-meaning employees who click, misconfigure, or share.

· Negligent insiders: bypassing policy (e.g., sharing credentials).

· Malicious insiders: intentional collusion.

· Social engineering of employees: impersonating executives (BEC), IT, or vendors.

Teaching point: Controls must account for human fallibility — defense-in-depth, least privilege, verification workflows.

3. Phishing-Specific Technical Subtopics

3.1 Email Authentication

Purpose: Understand the technical controls that verify sender legitimacy — and their limits.

· SPF (Sender Policy Framework): lists authorized sending IPs; does not survive forwarding well; does not prevent display-name spoofing.

· DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail): cryptographic signature; verifies message integrity and domain association.

· DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance): policy layer combining SPF/DKIM; enables reporting and rejection.

· Limitations: lookalike domains, compromised legitimate accounts, subdomain abuse, misconfigured policies.

3.2 URL and Domain Deception

Purpose: Recognize how attackers make malicious links appear legitimate.

· Typosquatting: paypa1.com, arnazon.com.

· Homoglyphs: visually similar characters (Cyrillic "а" vs. Latin "a").

· Subdomain tricks: bank.com.security-check.xyz.

· Open redirects: legitimate domain redirecting to attacker site.

· URL shorteners: hide destination.

· Punycode/IDN attacks: internationalized domain name confusion.

· HTTPS illusion: padlock icon does not guarantee legitimacy.

3.3 Credential Harvesting Pages

Purpose: Understand what happens after the click.

· Clone pages: pixel-perfect copies of login portals.

· Adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM): reverse proxy (e.g., Evilginx-style) that relays real authentication and steals session cookies — defeats many MFA implementations.

· Fake MFA prompts: capturing OTPs in real time.

· Post-harvest use: credential stuffing, account takeover, lateral movement.

3.4 Malicious Attachments

Purpose: Identify payload delivery mechanisms.

· Macro-enabled documents: Word/Excel prompting "enable content."

· HTML smuggling: encoded payload assembled in the browser.

· Archive files: ZIP/RAR/ISO containing executables or scripts.

· LNK and script files: shortcut and script-based execution.

· QR codes (quishing): move the attack to a personal device that may lack corporate protections.

3.5 Multi-Factor Authentication Attacks

Purpose: Understand that MFA is not a silver bullet.

· MFA fatigue / push bombing: repeated push notifications until approval.

· OTP interception: real-time phishing proxies capture one-time codes.

· SIM swapping: porting the victim’s number to intercept SMS OTPs.

· Session token theft: stealing cookies to bypass MFA entirely.

· Countermeasures: phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2, passkeys), number matching, conditional access.

3.6 Phishing Kits and Phishing-as-a-Service

Purpose: Understand the commoditization of attacks.

· Kits: pre-built page templates, credential exfiltration, evasion features.

· PhaaS: subscription-based access to infrastructure and kits.

· Features: CAPTCHA gating, geofencing, user-agent filtering, anti-sandbox checks, Telegram exfiltration bots.

· Implication: lowers skill barrier; increases scale and consistency.

3.7 Evasion Techniques

Purpose: Understand why detection is hard.

· CAPTCHA gating: blocks automated scanners.

· Geofencing: only shows malicious content to target regions.

· User-agent filtering: serves benign content to security tools.

· Sandbox detection: checks for virtualized environments.

· Polymorphism: unique URLs/content per recipient.

· Time-delayed payloads: activates after a delay to evade scanning.

4. Scam-Specific Typologies

4.1 Advance-Fee Fraud

Purpose: Understand the classic "pay to receive" structure.

· Mechanism: victim is promised a large sum but must pay fees/taxes upfront.

· Variants: inheritance scams, lottery scams, "business proposal" letters (419 scams).

· Persistence: staged payments escalate; sunk-cost fallacy keeps victims engaged.

· Modern forms: fake loans, fake grants, fake job offers requiring equipment fees.

4.2 Romance Scams

Purpose: Understand emotional grooming leading to financial exploitation.

· Stages: contact → rapport building → emotional dependency → crisis/request → repeated extraction.

· Platforms: dating apps, social media, gaming.

· Red flags: reluctance to video call, rapid declarations of love, requests for money for emergencies or travel.

· Overlap: often transitions into investment fraud ("pig-butchering").

4.3 Investment and Crypto Scams

Purpose: Understand fraudulent investment structures.

· Ponzi schemes: pay early investors with new investors' money.

· Rug pulls: developers abandon a crypto project after collecting funds.

· Pig-butchering (sha zhu pan): long-term grooming, fake trading platforms showing fictitious gains, escalating deposits, sudden lockout.

· Fake celebrity endorsements: deepfakes and impersonation.

· Red flags: guaranteed returns, pressure to recruit, unregistered platforms, withdrawal friction.

4.4 Marketplace and E-Commerce Scams

Purpose: Understand fraud in buying and selling contexts.

· Fake listings: goods that never arrive.

· Overpayment scams: "accidental" overpayment with request for refund of difference.

· Escrow fraud: fake escrow services.

· Non-delivery and misrepresentation: counterfeit goods, bait-and-switch.

· Account takeover: hijacked seller accounts with good reputations.

4.5 Impersonation Scams

Purpose: Understand identity-based deception across contexts.

· Government impersonation: tax authorities, police, social services.

· Tech support scams: fake warnings, remote access requests, "virus" claims.

· CEO fraud / BEC: executive impersonation to authorize payments.

· Family emergency scams: "grandparent scam," voice cloning.

· Utility and service impersonation: threats of disconnection.

4.6 Lottery, Prize, and Inheritance Scams

Purpose: Understand lure-based schemes exploiting excitement and greed.

· Mechanism: unexpected win requires fees, taxes, or information to "release" prize.

· Red flags: unsolicited winning notification, request for payment, urgency, secrecy.

· Overlap: frequently combined with advance-fee fraud.

5. Delivery Channels

5.1 Email Phishing

Purpose: Understand the most common vector.

· Mass phishing: broad campaigns, low sophistication, high volume.

· Spear phishing: tailored to individual, uses reconnaissance.

· Whaling: targets executives.

· Thread hijacking: uses compromised accounts to reply within existing conversations.

· Red flags: mismatched sender, urgency, unexpected attachments, link mismatch.

5.2 Smishing (SMS)

Purpose: Understand mobile-specific risks.

· Characteristics: short messages, shortened links, limited sender verification.

· Tactics: fake delivery notifications, bank alerts, MFA prompts, toll charges.

· Why effective: SMS feels personal; users check phones constantly; link previews are limited.

· Countermeasures: sender verification (where available), user education, link scanning.

5.3 Vishing (Voice)

Purpose: Understand phone-based social engineering.

· Tactics: caller-ID spoofing, IVR imitation, urgency, authority impersonation.

· Variants: tech support, bank fraud department, government agency, refund scams.

· Advanced: AI voice cloning for impersonation of family or colleagues.

· Countermeasures: call-back verification, employee training, number-blocking.

5.4 Social Media Phishing

Purpose: Understand abuse of social platforms.

· Tactics: fake profiles, direct messages, account takeover, malicious ads, fake giveaways.

· Platform-specific risks: trust derived from mutual connections, professional networks (LinkedIn), community groups.

· Countermeasures: privacy settings, reporting, verification of unexpected requests.

5.5 QR Phishing (Quishing)

Purpose: Understand the growing QR-code vector.

· Tactics: malicious QR codes in emails, physical posters, parking meters, restaurant menus.

· Why effective: moves victim to personal device; hides URL; bypasses email link scanners.

· Countermeasures: QR preview features, user caution, organizational policy.

5.6 Search-Engine Phishing

Purpose: Understand abuse of search and advertising.

· Tactics: malicious ads (malvertising), SEO poisoning, fake support pages.

· Mechanism: victim searches for a service, clicks a paid or optimized result, lands on attacker page.

· Countermeasures: ad verification, bookmarking trusted sites, browser protections.

5.7 Deepfake and Voice-Cloning Scams

Purpose: Understand synthetic media in impersonation.

· Tactics: AI-generated video/audio of executives, family members, or public figures.

· Use cases: authorizing payments, extracting information, fake endorsements.

· Countermeasures: verification protocols, code words, call-back procedures, media forensics.
